# SYSTEMATIC INEQUALITY

## Constitutional Promises, Minority Rights and the Struggle for Equal Citizenship in Pakistan

**By Riaz Anjum, Advocate High Court, President CLAP**

---

## Introduction

14 August 1973 is an important milestone in the constitutional and political history of Pakistan. On this day, the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan, 1973, came into force after being adopted through broad political consensus among the country’s major political forces. It established the framework for Pakistan’s political, legal and institutional order and set out fundamental rights and constitutional protections for its citizens.

The Constitution not only defines the powers and responsibilities of state institutions but also guarantees fundamental rights and freedoms. In particular, it contains important constitutional protections for religious and social minorities.

Yet, more than five decades after the Constitution came into force, a fundamental question remains:

> **If the Constitution guarantees equality, dignity, religious freedom and protection to all citizens, why do religious minorities continue to experience legal, political, social and institutional inequality?**

This question lies at the heart of Pakistan’s constitutional debate.

The issue is not simply whether discrimination exists at the individual or societal level. The deeper question is whether certain constitutional provisions, laws, administrative practices, institutional failures and political arrangements have combined over time to produce systematic and structural inequality for religious minorities.

---

## The Constitutional Promise of Equality

Several provisions of the Constitution establish fundamental rights that apply to citizens generally.

Article 9 protects life and liberty. Article 14 protects the dignity of man and, subject to law, the privacy of home. Article 15 guarantees freedom of movement and residence, Article 16 the right to peaceful assembly, Article 17 the right to form associations and political parties, and Article 19 guarantees freedom of speech and expression.

For religious minorities, Article 20 is of particular importance because it guarantees the right of every citizen to profess, practise and propagate his or her religion. It also gives every religious denomination and sect the right to establish, maintain and manage its religious institutions.

Similarly, Article 25 declares that all citizens are equal before the law and are entitled to equal protection of law. Article 26 prohibits discrimination in access to public places on grounds including race, religion, caste, sex, residence or place of birth. Article 27 provides protection against discrimination in appointments to the service of Pakistan on specified grounds, including religion.

The Constitution therefore establishes an important principle: citizenship should carry equal legal protection regardless of religious identity.

As Gankovsky and Moskalenko observed:

> “The Constitution declares the equality of all citizens before the law, regardless of their race, religion, caste or sex.”

— **Gankovsky, Y. V. and Moskalenko, V. N. (1978)**

The constitutional promise of equality is therefore clear. The difficulty lies in the gap between constitutional promise and constitutional reality.

---

## Article 20: Religious Freedom — A Right Subject to Conditions

The Constitution, on the one hand, guarantees religious freedom while, on the other hand, making that freedom subject to certain conditions.

Article 20 provides that every citizen has the right to profess, practise and propagate his religion and that every religious denomination and sect has the right to establish, maintain and manage its religious institutions.

However, the Article begins with an important qualification:

> “Subject to law, public order and morality.”

This qualification raises fundamental questions concerning the nature and limits of religious freedom.

In principle, religious freedom is regarded as a fundamental human right. Yet Article 20 presents the right within a constitutional framework that permits restrictions based on law, public order and morality.

When a fundamental freedom is made subject to broad concepts such as “law,” “public order” and “morality,” its practical scope can depend upon legislation, judicial interpretation, administrative practice and the prevailing political and social environment.

This raises an important question:

> **If the law itself places restrictions upon the propagation, practice or expression of a particular religion, to what extent does the freedom guaranteed by Article 20 remain effective?**

Similarly, concepts such as “public order” and “morality” may be interpreted differently in different circumstances. There is therefore a legitimate concern that majority social perceptions may, at times, influence the practical enjoyment of religious freedom by minority communities.

The Supreme Court itself, in its landmark 19 June 2014 judgment, emphasized the need to interpret Article 20 in a manner that protects religious freedom rather than allowing the right to become a means of restricting minority faiths.

Thus, Article 20 remains an important constitutional pillar of religious freedom, but it also raises a fundamental question:

> **Is religious freedom a complete constitutional guarantee, or is its practical enjoyment dependent upon the manner in which the State interprets and applies its limitations?**

---

## Article 25: Equality Before the Law and Inequality in Practice

Article 25 declares:

> “All citizens are equal before law and are entitled to equal protection of law.”

This is one of the most important constitutional guarantees in Pakistan.

But a difficult question follows:

> **Are minorities actually treated as equal citizens in practice?**

When minorities face discriminatory treatment; when minority students experience difficulties in educational institutions; when they face unequal treatment in admissions and classrooms; and when they are required to study material that conflicts with their religious identity, such circumstances raise serious questions about the implementation of the principle of equality.

The existence of a right in the Constitution does not automatically guarantee its enjoyment.

True equality requires more than identical language in a constitutional document. It requires equality in education, employment, access to justice, public institutions, political participation, security and everyday social life.

This is the distinction between **formal equality** and **substantive equality**.

**Formal equality** means that the law declares citizens equal.

**Substantive equality** requires that citizens actually experience that equality.

Where constitutional equality exists on paper but unequal outcomes persist systematically in practice, the issue becomes one of structural inequality.

---

## Article 27: Equal Opportunity in Public Employment

Article 27 provides that a citizen otherwise qualified for appointment to the service of Pakistan shall not be discriminated against on grounds including race, religion, caste, sex, residence or place of birth.

This provision is particularly important for religious minorities because access to public employment is an essential component of equal citizenship.

Pakistan has also adopted measures designed to ensure adequate representation of minorities in public employment. However, the existence of reserved opportunities has little practical value if posts remain vacant or if recruitment processes fail to reach minority candidates effectively.

The issue therefore goes beyond the mere existence of a quota.

The real question is whether the State is taking effective measures to ensure actual representation and equal opportunity.

When reserved minority positions remain vacant while thousands of citizens from minority communities remain excluded from public employment, the constitutional promise of equality and the State’s obligation to provide due representation under Article 36 are placed under serious question. Article 36 expressly requires the State to safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of minorities, including their due representation in Federal and Provincial services.

This is one of the ways in which inequality can become structural rather than merely individual.

---

## Article 36: The Constitutional Duty to Protect Minorities

Article 36 provides:

> “The State shall safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of minorities, including their due representation in the Federal and Provincial services.”

This is not merely a symbolic constitutional statement. It imposes a responsibility upon the State.

Yet Pakistan continues to face serious challenges concerning religious minorities, including forced conversions, religiously motivated violence, misuse of blasphemy allegations, discrimination and attacks on places of worship.

Such problems raise serious questions concerning the effective implementation of Article 36.

If the State fails to take effective preventive, administrative and legal measures to protect minorities, a constitutional guarantee risks becoming merely declaratory.

Constitutional protection must translate into effective policing, impartial investigation, prosecution, judicial protection, institutional safeguards and accountability.

---

## Articles 2 and 2-A: Religion and the Constitutional Structure

Article 2 declares Islam to be the State religion of Pakistan, while Article 2-A makes the principles and provisions of the Objectives Resolution a substantive part of the Constitution.

These provisions form an important part of Pakistan’s constitutional identity.

However, critics argue that the incorporation of Islamic principles into the constitutional structure has also contributed to greater influence of religious interpretations in legislation and state affairs.

The constitutional challenge is therefore not simply whether Pakistan should have a religious identity. The more difficult question is how that identity can coexist with the constitutional commitment to equal citizenship and the protection of citizens belonging to different faiths.

This debate becomes particularly significant when religious interpretations influence laws, public policy or administrative decisions in ways that may disproportionately affect religious minorities.

---

## Articles 41(2) and 91(3): Religious Qualifications for the Highest Offices

Another important dimension of the constitutional debate concerns the qualifications for the country’s two highest political offices.

Article 41(2) provides that a person shall not be eligible for election as President unless he is a Muslim.

Similarly, Article 91(3) requires the National Assembly to elect one of its Muslim members as Prime Minister.

The consequence is that citizens belonging to religious minority communities—including Christians, Hindus, Sikhs, Parsis, Baháʼís and other non-Muslim communities—cannot occupy the offices of President or Prime Minister.

This raises a fundamental question concerning equal citizenship.

> **If the Constitution declares that all citizens are equal before law, is it consistent with that principle to exclude citizens from the country’s highest constitutional offices solely because of their religion?**

This is not merely a political question. It is a constitutional question concerning the relationship between religious identity and citizenship.

The issue becomes even more significant when viewed against the broader constitutional promise of equality.

---

## The Supreme Court Judgment of 19 June 2014

One of the strongest examples of the gap between constitutional protection and institutional implementation is the historic judgment of the Supreme Court of Pakistan in **S.M.C. No. 1 of 2014**, delivered on **19 June 2014**.

The proceedings arose in the context of attacks on minorities, including the bombing of All Saints Church in Peshawar, and concerns regarding threats and discrimination against other religious communities.

The judgment was a landmark development in Pakistan’s constitutional jurisprudence concerning religious freedom and minority rights.

The Supreme Court issued directions relating to:

* protection of places of worship;
* protection of religious freedom;
* prevention of hate speech;
* establishment of a special police task force for the protection of minority places of worship;
* implementation of minority employment quotas;
* development of educational curricula promoting religious and social tolerance;
* establishment of a National Council for Minorities’ Rights; and
* mechanisms for monitoring implementation.

The Court also made clear that the practical enjoyment of minority rights was a matter requiring serious state attention.

The judgment therefore represented not merely a declaration of rights but a practical roadmap for the State.

---

## Twelve Years Later: The Problem of Non-Compliance

More than twelve years have now passed since the judgment.

According to Peter Jacob, writing in *The News on Sunday* on **28 June 2026**, substantive performance by the Federal and Provincial Governments regarding the Court’s directives had reached only **24 per cent compliance**. He described the implementation record as a continuing problem and noted that none of the governments during the preceding twelve years had constituted a dedicated body or assigned a ministry specifically to spearhead implementation of the judgment.

At the same time, Peter Jacob acknowledges that some progress has taken place. The Supreme Court’s one-man commission, established in 2019 to assist in assessing compliance, has processed hundreds of complaints, while some minority-focused police units have been established. His article also notes the recovery of communal properties belonging to Hindu and Sikh communities and other measures undertaken by state institutions.

This is important because the argument is not that nothing has been done.

The deeper concern is that implementation has remained incomplete, inconsistent and inadequate when measured against the constitutional and judicial commitments already made.

The 2014 judgment itself remains a powerful constitutional statement that minority rights are not privileges but rights requiring effective state protection.

---

## From Discrimination to Systematic Inequality

The problems faced by religious minorities in Pakistan therefore cannot be explained solely through individual prejudice or isolated incidents.

The larger picture includes:

* constitutional restrictions based on religion;
* unequal access to the highest political offices;
* gaps in the implementation of employment protections;
* educational concerns;
* inadequate protection of places of worship;
* religiously motivated violence;
* forced conversions;
* misuse of blasphemy allegations;
* institutional weaknesses; and
* incomplete implementation of judicial decisions.

When such problems repeatedly occur across different institutions and over extended periods, they raise the possibility of systematic and structural inequality.

Systematic inequality does not necessarily mean that every individual within the State discriminates against minorities.

It means that laws, institutions, policies, administrative practices or failures of implementation can interact in ways that consistently produce unequal outcomes for a particular community.

This distinction is crucial.

An individual act of discrimination is one problem.

But when discriminatory outcomes are repeatedly reproduced through institutions, laws, administrative practices or failures of enforcement, the problem becomes structural.

That is why the issue should not be reduced to individual prejudice.

The more important question is whether Pakistan’s constitutional and institutional framework is capable of delivering equal citizenship in practice.

---

## The Gap Between Constitutional Promise and Constitutional Reality

Pakistan’s Constitution contains strong guarantees of:

* equality;
* dignity;
* liberty;
* religious freedom; and
* protection of minorities.

At the same time, it contains provisions that:

* establish Islam as the State religion;
* incorporate the Objectives Resolution into the Constitution;
* restrict the offices of President and Prime Minister to Muslims; and
* subject religious freedom to law, public order and morality.

These provisions create a constitutional tension that deserves serious and open discussion.

The issue is not necessarily to deny Pakistan’s constitutional or religious identity.

Rather, the question is:

> **How can the constitutional identity of Pakistan be reconciled with the principle that every citizen should enjoy equal dignity, equal protection and equal citizenship?**

This is the central constitutional challenge.

---

## Equal Citizenship: The Way Forward

Today, as Pakistan continues its debate on democracy, human rights and constitutional reform, an important question must be confronted:

> **Should the rights and political opportunities of Pakistani citizens be determined by their religious identity, or should citizenship itself be the primary basis of political and legal equality?**

This question concerns much more than religious minorities.

It concerns the character of the Pakistani State, the meaning of democracy and the future of constitutionalism.

A democratic Constitution must not only protect the majority. It must also provide effective protection to citizens who are numerically weaker.

The strength of a constitutional democracy can ultimately be measured by how effectively it protects those who have the least political power.

For religious minorities, equal citizenship means more than constitutional language.

It means:

* security of life and property;
* freedom of worship;
* freedom to maintain religious identity;
* equal educational opportunities;
* equal access to employment;
* meaningful political participation;
* protection of places of worship;
* access to justice; and
* equal treatment before the law.

A constitutional promise becomes meaningful only when state institutions enforce it without discrimination.

---

## Conclusion

The Constitution of Pakistan 1973 contains both a promise of equality and constitutional provisions that raise difficult questions concerning the extent of that equality.

The fundamental issue is therefore not whether Pakistan’s Constitution contains protections for minorities. It clearly does.

The real question is whether those protections are effectively implemented and experienced in the daily lives of citizens.

If constitutional guarantees exist but remain inaccessible in practice; if judicial judgments remain incompletely implemented; if employment opportunities reserved for minorities remain unfilled; if religious freedom is constrained by discriminatory practices; if citizens are constitutionally excluded from the highest offices because of their religion; and if minorities continue to face violence, forced conversions, misuse of blasphemy allegations and attacks on their places of worship, then the principle of equal citizenship remains incomplete.

Pakistan therefore faces a fundamental constitutional choice.

It can continue to tolerate the gap between equality in constitutional language and inequality in lived reality, or it can undertake meaningful legal, institutional and political reforms to ensure that citizenship becomes genuinely equal.

The future of Pakistan’s religious minorities depends upon this choice.

But ultimately, the issue is larger than minority rights.

It is about the kind of State Pakistan seeks to be.

It is about whether citizenship is a common legal identity shared equally by all Pakistanis, or whether religious identity determines the extent of constitutional rights and political participation.

It is about whether democracy means merely majority rule, or whether it also means the protection of minorities.

And it is about whether the Constitution of 1973 will remain primarily a document of promises or become a living instrument of equal citizenship, justice and human dignity.

The true test of Pakistan’s constitutional democracy will not be found merely in the words written in the Constitution. It will be found in whether those words are translated into the lives of every citizen.

The journey from constitutional promise to equal citizenship requires Pakistan to confront systematic inequality—not merely as a social problem, but as a constitutional challenge.
